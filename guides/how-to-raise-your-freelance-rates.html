How to Raise Your Freelance Rates With Existing Clients

Raising your rate with a client who already pays you feels riskier than setting a rate with someone new, but it is a normal, manageable part of running a freelance business. Here is when to do it, how much to ask for, what to actually write, and what to do when a client pushes back.

When to raise your rate

Four signals tell you it is time, and any one of them is enough on its own.

  • You are booked out. If your calendar is full weeks ahead and you are turning down other work to keep serving current clients, your price is below what the market will pay for your time. Demand exceeding supply is the clearest pricing signal there is.
  • The scope has grown. Clients rarely announce that a project has expanded. A monthly blog post picks up social captions and a newsletter without anyone deciding that on purpose. Compare what you deliver today against what you agreed to at the original rate, and if the list has grown, the rate should grow with it.
  • The market has moved. Rates for your skill and experience level shift over time, usually upward. If new clients are paying more than your existing ones for comparable work, your long-standing clients are getting a discount they never asked for and probably do not expect.
  • A year has passed. Even with no other signal, a full year at the same rate is reason enough. Costs rise, your skills improve, and a rate that felt fair twelve months ago is falling behind without you noticing.

None of these require the client to have done anything wrong. A rate increase is routine business maintenance, not a response to a problem.

How much to raise it

For an existing client you plan to keep, 10 to 20 percent is the normal range. It is large enough to matter over a year and small enough that most clients absorb it without much thought. Go toward the lower end for a client you renew often, and toward the higher end when scope has visibly grown or a full year or more has passed since the last change.

For brand-new clients, price at your current market rate from the start, even if that is a bigger jump from last year's number than you would ever ask an existing client to absorb. New clients have no anchor to your old number, so there is no reason to under-price them to match it. A 40 or 50 percent gap between what your newest client pays and what your oldest client pays is a sign the increases have not kept pace.

Run the numbers with the freelance rate calculator before you settle on a figure, or check your current rate against a profession-specific rate calculator.

How to tell a client

Keep the notice short and in writing, so there is a record and the client has time to plan. A few rules make this land well:

  • Give 30 to 60 days' notice. Enough time for the client to plan around it. Less feels abrupt, especially for anyone billing through a company that needs approvals.
  • Set a clear effective date. Not "soon," a specific date. "Starting November 1" removes ambiguity and any temptation to negotiate the timing.
  • Frame it around value and continuity, not your costs or your feelings. The client does not need to know your rent went up, only that the rate reflects the work and nothing else about the relationship is changing.
  • Do not apologize. An apology signals that you think the increase is unreasonable, which invites the client to agree with you.
  • Keep it short. One clear paragraph beats three defensive ones.

Scripts for the message

A short email beats a call for most clients: it gives them a written reference and does not put them on the spot to react immediately. Adjust the specifics, but the shape below covers most cases.

General notice: "Hi [name], I wanted to give you advance notice that my rate is increasing from [old rate] to [new rate], effective [date]. This reflects [the scope we've added / where my rate sits in the market / the past year of work together]. Everything else about how we work stays the same. Let me know if you'd like to talk it through before then."

Scope-driven notice: "Hi [name], looking back over the last few months, our work together has grown to include [list]. Starting [date], I'm updating my rate to [new rate] to reflect that. Happy to walk through what's included if useful."

Retainer renewal notice: "Hi [name], as we come up on renewal, I wanted to flag that the retainer will be [new rate] per month going forward, covering [scope]. If that doesn't fit your budget, I'm glad to look at a smaller scope that keeps the retainer at the current price."

Notice the pattern: state the number, name the date, give a short reason, and leave the door open without sounding uncertain about the decision itself.

Handling pushback

Most clients say nothing, or reply with a one-line "sounds good." When a client does push back, you have three honest options, and all three are better than caving on the number and saying nothing.

  • Grandfather them briefly. For a client you want to keep, offer one more cycle or one more project at the old rate before the new one starts. Set a firm end date so it does not become the new normal by default.
  • Reduce scope instead of price. If the budget truly will not stretch, keep your new rate and shrink what it buys: fewer revisions, a narrower deliverable, less frequent check-ins. This protects your rate and gives the client an honest choice instead of a discount that leaves one of you resenting the arrangement.
  • Let a low-value client go. If a client consistently pays the least, asks for the most, and resists every increase, the rate conversation is often the moment that relationship was heading toward anyway. Letting them go frees time for clients who pay your current rate without friction. The guide to finding freelance clients covers how to refill that space.

What you should not do is hold the old rate indefinitely because asking felt uncomfortable once. That discomfort is a one-time cost, far smaller than a year of underpriced work. If a client counters with a specific percentage, put it through the discount calculator before you answer. Once your costs come out, the drop in what you keep is larger than the drop in the price, and seeing that number makes the reply easier to write.

Raising retainers at renewal

Retainers are easier to raise than project work because they already have a natural pause point: the renewal date. Send the new terms ahead of that date rather than mid-cycle, and tie the new number to what the retainer now covers rather than presenting it as a flat bump out of nowhere. A retainer that has absorbed extra requests over the year without a price change deserves a rate that reflects the current scope, not the scope from when it started.

The retainer pricing calculator lets you price the renewed scope from scratch instead of adjusting last year's number by feel. If the client cannot meet the new retainer, offer a smaller monthly scope at the current price rather than holding the old rate for the full scope.

A worked example

Say you have billed a client $75 an hour for eighteen months. Over that time you have added a monthly reporting task they never paid extra for, your other clients now pay closer to $90, and you have not revisited the number once.

On a client billing 10 hours a month, that increase is $120 extra a month, or about $1,440 a year, for work you are already doing. If the client had pushed back and asked to hold at $75, the fallback would be to drop the reporting task back out of scope rather than absorb it for free. Either outcome beats the current arrangement of doing more work for the old price.

A simple yearly review habit

Pick one date, such as the start of the year or your business anniversary, and put a recurring reminder on it. On that date, review every active client: what you charge, what has changed in scope, and what comparable clients or the market now pay. Decide for each one whether the rate holds, rises, or the relationship should end, and send any notices at once rather than piecemeal through the year.

Freelancers who fall behind on pricing are rarely the ones who asked for too little at the start. They are the ones who set a rate once and never looked at it again. Frequently asked questions

How much notice should I give before a rate increase?

Give 30 to 60 days for most clients, and closer to 60 or 90 for retainer clients who have their own budget cycles to work around. Enough notice for the client to plan removes the surprise, which is usually what causes pushback, not the increase itself.

Should I apologize for raising my rate?

No. An apology frames the increase as something you did to the client rather than a normal part of running a business, and it invites negotiation. State the new rate plainly, give the effective date, and thank them for the work. Confidence reads as normal; apology reads as negotiable.

What if a client says they can't afford the new rate?

Offer a smaller scope at the old rate instead of holding the price. You keep your new rate for the work you actually do, the client keeps a version of the relationship that fits their budget, and neither of you ends up resentful about it. If no smaller scope works for either side, it may be time to let that client go.

Is it different to raise a retainer rate versus a project rate?

The mechanics are similar, but retainers renew on a schedule, so the increase belongs at the renewal point rather than mid-term. Send the new terms before the renewal date, tie the number to what the retainer now covers, and let the client decide to renew, adjust scope, or step away with a clean end date.

How often should I actually raise my rates?

At least once a year, even if the increase is small, so no single jump feels drastic. Raise sooner than the annual review if you are consistently booked out, turning down work, or your skills have clearly grown since you last set the number.
