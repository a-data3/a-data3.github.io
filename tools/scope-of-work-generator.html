What a scope of work should always include

A scope of work (SOW) is the document that says exactly what you will deliver, by when, and for how much. Just as importantly, it says what you will not do. It's the reference you point to when a project starts drifting. A strong freelance SOW has six parts:

  1. Overview: the goal in a sentence or two, so both sides agree on the point of the work.
  2. Deliverables: a specific, itemised list. "A website" is vague enough to cause a dispute later. "Homepage + 4 inner pages, responsive build, contact form" leaves much less room for argument.
  3. Timeline: the duration or milestones, and what you need from the client to hit them.
  4. Revisions: how many rounds are included. Leaving revisions unlimited is how fixed-price projects end up losing money.
  5. Exclusions: what is explicitly out of scope. This section on its own prevents most scope creep, because "just one more thing" now has a written answer.
  6. Fee and payment terms: the total, the payment schedule (ask for a deposit), and how change requests are charged.

Exclusions are the most valuable lines

Freelancers instinctively list what they'll do and forget to say what they won't. The exclusions section is where you protect yourself. If copywriting, photography, maintenance, or extra revisions are named as out of scope, adding them later becomes a paid change order instead of an awkward argument. When a client asks for something on the exclusions list, you can reply "Happy to, that would be a change request." Over a year of projects, that one reply keeps a lot of work paid that would otherwise have been done for free.

Frequently asked questions

Is a scope of work legally binding?

An SOW becomes binding when it's incorporated into a signed contract or accepted in writing. On its own it is a clear statement of intent. To make it enforceable, attach it to a contract and get written agreement.

What's the difference between an SOW and a contract?

The contract sets the legal terms: liability, IP, termination. The SOW defines the specific work, deliverables, and price. They work together, and the SOW is usually attached to the contract as an exhibit.

How detailed should deliverables be?

Detailed enough that a stranger could read the list and tell whether you delivered each item. Vague deliverables tend to end in disputes, while specific ones make it much easier to get signed off and paid.
