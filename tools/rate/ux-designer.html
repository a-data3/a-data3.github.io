In 2026, freelance UX designers commonly charge roughly $60–$150+ per hour, with mid-level generalists around $75–$110 and senior or specialized (fintech, enterprise, research-led) designers going well beyond $150, though this varies significantly by region, industry, and experience.

What actually moves a UX designer's rate

Your rate is set less by your Figma skills than by how close you sit to the client's money and how much ambiguity you can absorb. A designer who takes a vague "we need to redesign onboarding" and returns a researched, tested flow with measurable drop-off improvements commands two to three times the rate of one who waits for tickets. The specific levers:

  • Domain complexity. Consumer marketing sites are the cheap end. Fintech, healthcare, B2B SaaS, enterprise dashboards, and data-heavy or regulated products pay far more because the design problems are harder and the cost of getting them wrong is higher.
  • Research ownership. Designers who run discovery, interviews, and usability testing (not just push pixels) charge a premium. If you can say "I'll validate this before we build it," you are de-risking an engineering budget, and that is worth a lot.
  • Business proximity. Tie your work to activation, conversion, retention, or support-ticket reduction and you stop competing on hourly rate entirely.
  • Design systems and dev handoff. Building or extending a component library, writing clean specs, and speaking engineers' language shortens build time. Teams pay for that fluency.
  • Seniority and portfolio proof. Case studies that show outcomes, not just screens, raise your rate more than anything else.

Which pricing model fits UX work

Hourly is the honest choice for open-ended, exploratory, or advisory work where scope cannot be pinned down: early discovery, ongoing tweaks, or "sit in our design reviews." Use it as your fallback and your floor, not your default. The trap is that hourly caps your income at your capacity and punishes you for getting faster.

Project or fixed-fee fits most defined deliverables: an app redesign, an onboarding flow, a design-system starter, a website UX overhaul. Price the outcome and the phases (discovery, IA, wireframes, hi-fi, prototype, handoff), not the hours. Always scope the number of rounds of revisions and the number of key screens explicitly, or scope creep eats your margin. A good fixed fee lets a fast, experienced designer earn an effective hourly rate far above what they could bill directly.

Retainer is the option most UX designers overlook. A monthly retainer of a set number of days for an ongoing product team gives you predictable income and gives them continuity of design thinking. Startups without a full-time designer love it, and it is the model most likely to turn into a stable, well-paid relationship. Value-based pricing is the top tier: when you can credibly tie your work to revenue (a checkout redesign on a site doing serious monthly volume), price against the impact, not your time.

The classic mistake

The mistake UX designers make over and over is pricing the deliverables instead of the decisions, and letting "just one more screen" or "can we try another direction" slide because it feels small. You quoted for a 12-screen app; now it is 20 screens, three extra stakeholders, and a fourth revision round, all at the original price. Fix it with a written scope that names the deliverables, the number of screens, and the number of revision rounds, plus a simple change-order rate for anything beyond it. The second half of the same mistake is invisible research: doing interviews and testing but never showing the client how those reduced their risk, so they see you as a Figma operator and haggle accordingly.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average ux designer rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This calculates the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use any published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Start realistic; a too-high number sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients buy outcomes, not hours.
