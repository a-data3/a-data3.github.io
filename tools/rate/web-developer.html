In 2026, freelance web developers in the US typically charge between $50 and $150 per hour, with a median around $85-$105. Rates vary a lot by region, stack, and experience, and senior specialists in high-demand niches often charge $150-$250+.

What moves a web developer's rate

Two developers with the same tech stack can charge very different rates. The difference usually comes down to who they work for and what they take responsibility for. Three things matter most. First, whether you own the outcome (a store that converts, a page that loads fast) or just work through tickets. Second, how much is riding on the site: a solo founder's brochure site is a different job from an e-commerce platform doing six figures a month. Third, how much of the stack you cover. Full-stack developers who can handle infrastructure, a headless CMS, payments, and a CI pipeline charge 20-40% more than front-end-only builders, because the client doesn't have to assemble a team around you.

Specialization pays more than seniority alone. Clients can find a "React developer" anywhere, so they compare on price. A "Shopify Plus developer who has migrated a store off a legacy platform without losing SEO" is much harder to replace. The narrower your niche, and the closer it sits to the client's revenue, the less you compete on price.

Which pricing model fits web work

Hourly billing is where most developers start, and it works for open-ended maintenance or discovery work where the scope can't be pinned down in advance. The problem is that it punishes you for getting faster. The better you know a stack, the fewer hours each build takes, and the less you earn from it.

  • Project pricing is the usual choice for defined builds (a marketing site, a redesign, a store setup). Quote a flat fee for the deliverable rather than for the hours. You keep the benefit of your speed, and a fixed number is much easier for a client to approve than an open meter. It only works when the scope is written down and you have past builds to estimate from, so protect it with a clear statement of work.
  • Retainers are the option most developers overlook. Nearly every site you launch needs ongoing updates, security patches, plugin maintenance, and small features. Package that as a fixed monthly fee (commonly $1,500-$5,000) for a set block of work. It evens out the feast-or-famine cycle and turns one-off clients into predictable revenue. Price the retainer close to your project rate rather than below it.
  • Value-based pricing applies when the site directly drives revenue, such as a checkout rebuild that lifts conversion. In that case, price against a share of the upside rather than against your hours.

The mistake that drags web developers' rates down

It's under-scoping the job and then absorbing the extra work for free. A client approves a "5-page site," then asks for a blog, a contact form integration, a newsletter signup, three rounds of "just one more tweak," and content you assumed they'd provide. Each request feels small, so you say yes, and a $6,000 project turns into 90 hours of work at an effective $40/hour. Working faster won't fix this. What fixes it is a written scope that names the deliverables, sets revision limits, says who supplies content, and states a change-order rate for anything outside it. "Happy to add that, it's a small change order" is one of the most profitable sentences you can learn to say.

A related trap is quoting before discovery. Never price a build from a two-line brief. Run a short paid discovery session, or build a discovery phase into the quote, so your number rests on the real requirements instead of an optimistic guess.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average web developer rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This works out the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Be realistic here. If you overestimate your hours, the calculator will set your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients care about the finished site far more than about how many hours it took you.
