Freelance copywriter rate calculator

Copywriting rates swing wider than almost any other freelance field. A blog post and a sales page are both "just words," but one might pay $150 and the other $6,000. What separates them is how much money the copy is expected to move. Word count has little to do with it. In 2026 most freelance copywriters bill roughly $50–$150/hour (averaging around $70), with conversion and B2B specialists reaching $160–$300+. Your real number depends heavily on region, niche, and proven results. Most experienced copywriters also quote per project or per word rather than by the hour.

What sets a copywriter's rate

Two copywriters can both write "500 words" and charge ten times apart. The gap is rarely talent on the page. Usually it comes down to what the words are hired to do. A batch of product descriptions for an e-commerce store and a cold-email sequence for a B2B SaaS pipeline are priced by completely different logic. Before you quote, work out where your job sits on each of these:

  • How directly it touches revenue. Conversion assets such as sales pages, VSLs, email launch sequences, ad copy, and landing pages command the top rates. Copy one step removed (blog posts, SEO articles, social captions) pays a fraction of that, because the buyer can't draw a straight line from your sentence to a sale.
  • Niche and jargon depth. Generalists compete with everyone, and now with AI tools as well. Specialists in fintech, health, cybersecurity, or B2B SaaS charge a premium because clients are paying for the research shortcut and for the credibility of someone who already speaks their market's language.
  • Proof. "I wrote this email and it drove $80k" resets your rate faster than any years-of-experience figure. Testimonials and results that beat the client's existing control are the strongest lever you have.
  • Stakes and deadline. Copy for a $2M product launch is not priced like a founder's personal blog, even at the same word count. Rush turnarounds and heavy strategy or research work rightly add to the number.

Which pricing model fits copywriting

For most copywriters, hourly is the wrong default, and it gets worse as you improve. Once you can write a great subject line in ten minutes instead of an hour, hourly billing pays you less for being more skilled. Use one of these instead:

Per project (flat fee)

This is the model most copywriters use most of the time. You quote one price for the whole sales page, email sequence, or website. That protects your speed, gives the client a fixed number, and lets you build research and revisions into the fee. Anchor the fee to what the asset is worth, then divide it by the hours it will realistically take and check the result against the hourly floor described below.

Value-based

For high-stakes conversion work where you can estimate the outcome, such as a launch funnel or a webinar sequence, price against a share of the value the copy creates rather than the time it takes. This is where senior direct-response copywriters reach $5,000–$20,000+ for a single asset.

Retainer

For ongoing work like a weekly newsletter, a monthly content batch, or an email program, a fixed monthly fee gives you predictable income and gives the client priority access. Price it as a bundle of project fees with a modest discount. Don't price it as a discounted hourly rate.

The classic mistake

The mistake that keeps copywriters underpaid is pricing the deliverable instead of the outcome. They count words, estimate hours, add a margin, and send the quote, treating a sales page that will run for two years as if it were a commodity. Related traps include charging content-mill per-word rates, giving away strategy and research for free, and quoting before you understand the business goal. One habit fixes all of these: ask what the copy is worth to the client before you name a price. Also, never send a quote with unlimited revisions attached. Cap it (two rounds is standard) so a $2,000 project can't balloon into a $6,000 one.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average copywriter rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This calculates the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Start with a realistic figure. If you set it too high, your rate comes out too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients care about the outcome far more than the hours behind it.
