In 2026, most freelance podcast editors charge somewhere between $15 and $60 per finished audio hour for basic cleanup, or roughly $50 to $400 or more per episode for fuller multi-track production with extras, though the exact number varies a lot by region, experience, and how messy the raw files are.

Who actually hires a podcast editor

Most work comes from three types of clients. Solo hosts and small creators who record on a phone or a basic USB mic and need someone to clean up the mess and cut the show to time. Businesses and marketing teams running a branded podcast as a growth channel, where the budget is steadier and expectations for polish and show notes are higher. And production agencies or small networks that hire editors as subcontractors on a roster of shows, usually at a lower per-episode rate but with more consistent volume.

What actually changes the price

The finished runtime of an episode is the least useful number for pricing, because it hides the real cost driver: how much raw material you're working from and how it was recorded.

  • Track count. A single mixed-down file from a phone recorder is far less work than four or five separate tracks from a multi-mic session that need syncing, individual leveling, and noise treatment on each voice.
  • Raw-to-finished ratio. A two-hour raw conversation cut down to a 40-minute episode takes real editorial judgment, not just noise removal. That's different work from trimming ten minutes of dead air off an already-tight recording.
  • Level of polish. Cutting obvious mistakes and long pauses is one job. A full narrative edit with music beds, sound design, and pacing work for a scripted show is another, and it can take three or four times as long per finished minute.
  • Extras bundled in. Show notes, chapter markers, an SEO-friendly title and description, audiograms or short clips for social, and a full transcript are each separate deliverables even when clients treat them as part of editing.
  • Video. Editing the video version for YouTube, cutting cross-cuts between speakers, adding captions and lower thirds, is a different skill and time commitment from the audio edit, and should be priced as its own line item.
  • Turnaround and cadence. A weekly show with a 24-hour turnaround deserves a rush premium or a retainer that reserves your time. An occasional one-off does not.

Which pricing model fits

Per-episode flat fees work best for most podcast editing, because the client can budget against a known number and you're not penalized for getting faster with practice or better tools. Quote it after seeing a sample of the raw audio, since track count and recording quality change the real workload more than runtime does.

Per-minute-of-finished-audio pricing is a common way to explain that flat fee to clients and scale it cleanly when an episode runs long, typically split into a base cleanup rate and a higher rate for heavier production work.

Retainers make sense once a client is recurring, usually a weekly or biweekly show. A retainer covering a fixed number of episodes a month, with clear scope per episode and an overage rate for anything beyond it, protects your calendar and gives the client priority scheduling.

Hourly billing is the weakest fit for this work. Editing speed depends on your tools, your presets, and how many times you've edited a particular host's style, so an hourly rate rewards slowness and makes clients nervous about a number that changes every invoice.

The mistake that costs editors money

The classic error is quoting a price from the finished-episode length alone, without asking how many tracks the raw files come in or how messy the recording is, then discovering on delivery that the quick edit needs manual leveling on four microphones, gap-filling for a laptop fan, and three passes to hit a coherent pace. The fix: ask for a short raw sample before quoting, name the track count and rough raw length in the agreement, and list show notes, clips, and transcripts as add-ons with their own price rather than assumed inclusions. Frequently asked questions

Is this the average podcast editor rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This works out the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Published averages are useful only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20 to 30 hours a week once sales, admin, and unpaid work are taken out. Start with a realistic figure, because a number that is too high sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients buy outcomes rather than hours.
