In 2026, freelance WordPress developers typically charge $40 to $80 per hour at mid-level and $100 to $175 per hour as senior specialists, though rates vary widely by niche (WooCommerce and custom plugins command more), region, and client type.

What actually moves a WordPress developer's rate

Niche is the first lever on your rate, and it matters more than your speed with a theme. A generalist who "builds WordPress sites" competes with a global marketplace and lands around $40 to $80 an hour. A specialist who says "I fix slow WooCommerce checkouts" or "I build custom Gutenberg blocks and ACF-driven sites" is comparing against almost no one. That's why senior specialists command $100 to $175 an hour in 2026, while the generalist middle stays compressed. WooCommerce, security hardening, performance optimization, and custom plugin work each add a 15 to 40 percent premium because they carry real business risk, and clients pay to make that risk go away.

The second lever is who you serve. A local restaurant wants a brochure site and has a $1,500 ceiling in their head. A SaaS company or an agency subcontracting overflow work has a budget line for "development" and evaluates you on reliability, not price. The same skills can land wildly different rate ceilings depending on who's paying. If your calendar is full of five-page small-business sites, your rate problem is a client-selection problem.

Which pricing model fits your work

WordPress work splits cleanly into three modes, and each wants a different model:

  • Project pricing for anything with a defined scope: a new build, a WooCommerce store, a theme migration. Hourly punishes you here, because your efficiency (the block patterns, starter theme, and staging workflow you've refined over years) becomes a reason to bill fewer hours. Price the outcome. A small business site is $2,000 to $5,000; a WooCommerce store is $1,500 to $6,000; a custom plugin runs $1,000 to $10,000 and up.
  • Hourly or day rate for open-ended work where you can't see the bottom: rescue jobs, "the previous developer disappeared" cleanups, debugging a white screen of death on a site with forty plugins. Charge a premium here. Emergency and inherited-mess work should never be at your comfortable project-equivalent rate.
  • Monthly retainer for care plans and ongoing partnerships. This is the most underused model in WordPress and the one that builds a stable income. Updates, backups, uptime and security monitoring, and a bucket of small changes bundle into $500 to $2,000 a month for basic plans, and $2,000 to $5,000+ for active development retainers. Price retainers on response-time promises, not just task lists: same-day turnaround is worth far more than a 48-hour window.

The classic mistake

WordPress developers systematically underprice ongoing maintenance and give away support. You finish a $4,000 build, and then spend the next two years answering "can you just update this real quick" emails for free because it feels rude to bill fifteen minutes. That unpaid support is often larger than the original project fee, spread invisibly across your year. Every launched site should convert into a care plan at handoff, or your support boundary should be written into the contract in plain language. The second version of this mistake is quoting from the happy path: you price the site as if the client will deliver clean content on time, then eat the days lost to their stock-photo hunt, their three rounds of "make the logo bigger," and their staging feedback that arrives as phone screenshots. Build a content-delay and revision-limit clause into every project, or bake that slack into the number.

Using the calculator

Start with the income you need and your real billable hours, not a 40-hour fantasy week; between sales, admin, and unbillable learning, most solo WordPress developers bill 20 to 25 hours. The calculator back-solves the hourly floor you can't go below. Then treat that floor as the input to your project quotes: estimate the hours honestly, add your risk buffer, and quote a fixed number so your efficiency stays yours.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average wordpress developer rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This calculates the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use any published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Start realistic; a too-high number sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients buy outcomes, not hours.
