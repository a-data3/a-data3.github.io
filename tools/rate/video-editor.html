In 2026, freelance video editors typically charge about $25–$150 per hour, or per-video from roughly $25–$500 for short-form and $150–$2,500 for long-form, though this varies widely by region, niche, and experience.

How video editors should actually price their work

Video editing is one of the few creative trades where getting faster and more skilled can lower your income if you price it wrong. If you bill by the hour, every shortcut you master and every process you streamline just means earning less for the same edit. The clients paying you rarely care how long an edit took; they care that the finished video keeps viewers watching, sells the product, or captures the day. Price the outcome and the scope, not the clock.

What really moves your rate

Editors serve wildly different clients: YouTube creators, marketing agencies, corporate comms teams, course creators, podcasters wanting clips, real estate and e-commerce brands, and couples booking a wedding. Across all of them, the same variables drive what you can charge:

  • Raw-to-final ratio. Ten minutes of finished video from 45 minutes of footage is a different job than the same ten minutes pulled from six hours of multicam, unsynced audio, and B-roll.
  • Turnaround. A 24–48 hour rush should carry a 25–50% premium; it displaces other work and kills your weekend.
  • Deliverable count. One master video plus five vertical cutdowns in three aspect ratios means five extra exports, five sets of captions, and five re-frames. It is not "the same video" five times.
  • Technical lift. Motion graphics, color grading, sound design, and animated captions are line items, not freebies baked into a base rate.
  • Usage. An ad running paid media is worth more than an organic upload; price for where the video lives.

Hourly vs per-video vs retainer

For most creator and brand work, flat per-video (per-deliverable) pricing wins. It rewards your speed, makes budgets predictable for the client, and lets you scope revisions and footage up front. In 2026, short-form edits under 60 seconds run roughly $25–$75 (beginner) to $200–$500 (senior), while a polished 10–20 minute long-form YouTube edit ranges from $150–$300 up to $800–$2,500 depending on retention editing, graphics, and complexity.

Retainers are the goal for any ongoing channel: commonly $1,500–$3,500/month at the intermediate tier and $5,000–$10,000 at the senior tier for four long-form videos plus social cutdowns. Retainers trade a 20–40% per-video discount for guaranteed volume and cash-flow stability; only offer them once you know your true per-video time. Reserve hourly for work you truly can't scope in advance, like messy archival projects or live event same-day cuts.

The mistake that caps your income

Billing hourly isn't the only problem here. Quoting a per-video price without capping revision rounds or defining footage volume does just as much damage. "Unlimited revisions" and "oh, can you also make a version for TikTok" are where your effective rate collapses to minimum wage. Write "two revision rounds, up to X minutes of source footage; additional rounds and deliverables billed at [rate]" into every quote. That one sentence protects the margin the calculator helps you set.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average video editor rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This calculates the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use any published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Start realistic; a too-high number sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients buy outcomes, not hours.
