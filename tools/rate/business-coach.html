In 2026, business coaches typically charge roughly $150–$500 per hour (senior and executive-focused coaches often $500–$1,500), or $500–$3,000+ per month on retainer, though rates vary widely by niche, region, client revenue, and demonstrated results.

What actually moves a business coach's rate

Business coaching is priced on proximity to money. A coach helping a $300k solopreneur untangle their offer commands a different rate than one helping a $3M founder build a leadership team, even if both meet for the same hour. The three levers that move your number most:

  • The client's revenue and the size of the problem. A 10% lift is worth far more to a $2M business than a $150k one. Coaches who work with established owners can and should charge multiples of what a startup-stage coach charges.
  • Provable results. Vague "clarity and confidence" language keeps you at the bottom of the range. Concrete before-and-afters justify premium fees: revenue added, a first hire made, 15 hours a week reclaimed, a business made sellable. Your ICF or ICF-adjacent credential matters far less than your case studies.
  • Niche specificity. "Business coach" is a commodity. "Coach for agency owners scaling from six to seven figures" is a category of one. The tighter your niche, the less price resistance you meet.

Which pricing model fits

Hourly billing is the weakest model for this field, and most experienced coaches abandon it. A single one-hour session often represents three hours of real work once you count prep, notes, and between-session support. Hourly framing also invites clients to buy fewer, shorter sessions, which undercuts the very consistency that produces results.

Packages and retainers win

Transformation takes months, so price in months. Multi-session packages (a 3- or 6-month engagement sold as one figure) and monthly retainers (typically two to four sessions plus messaging access between calls) are the dominant models because they align payment with the arc of change and stabilize your income. Retainers also let you price on compounding value rather than resetting the meter every session. Reserve pure hourly rates for one-off strategy intensives or VIP days.

The classic mistake

The most common error is pricing on your own comfort instead of the client's ROI: quoting a number you'd feel okay paying, rather than one anchored to the outcome the client is buying. Close cousins to watch for:

  • Selling single sessions when the result requires an engagement, then blaming the client when one call doesn't stick.
  • Discounting to close a nervous prospect, which signals your own doubt and attracts clients who won't do the work.
  • Raising rates by tiny increments for years while your results have doubled.
  • Never accounting for unpaid hours, the discovery calls, proposals, marketing, and admin that can eat half your week and must be covered by billable rates.

How to use this calculator

Enter your target income, the weeks you'll actually work, and an honest estimate of how many hours are truly billable versus spent on sales and admin. The calculator back-solves the hourly rate that hits your goal. Then translate it. If it returns $250/hour and you run four sessions a month, that's a $1,000 retainer floor before you price in the value you create or the between-session access clients lean on. Treat the output as your baseline, then adjust upward for niche, proof, and the revenue level of the businesses you serve.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average business coach rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This calculates the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use any published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Start realistic; a too-high number sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients are paying for the outcome you deliver.
