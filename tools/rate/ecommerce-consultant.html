Freelance e-commerce consultants typically bill between $75 and $200 an hour in 2026, with specialists in Amazon marketplace strategy or Shopify Plus migrations often charging $150 to $300 or more, though actual rates vary widely by region, platform expertise, and years of experience.

What moves the rate

A CRO audit on an existing Shopify store carries a different risk than a full replatform from Magento to BigCommerce, and clients feel that difference when they compare quotes. A few factors set most of the number.

  • Platform depth: certified Shopify Plus or Adobe Commerce experience commands more than general WooCommerce or Wix work, since enterprise platforms carry more configuration risk and higher-revenue clients.
  • Marketplace specialization: Amazon Seller Central and Vendor Central work (listing optimization, PPC management, brand registry disputes) is priced separately from owned-site work, because that knowledge changes every few months.
  • Timing: work scheduled around Black Friday and Cyber Monday carries a premium, since a mistake made that week costs the client far more than the same mistake in February.
  • Scope of access: consultants who touch checkout, payment gateways, or inventory sync take on more liability than consultants doing pure strategy, and rates should reflect that.
  • Client stakes: a fifty-thousand-dollar-a-month brand and a fifty-million-dollar-a-year retailer are buying the same skill set at very different dollar risk, so fees should scale with what a mistake or a win is worth to the client.

Which pricing model fits

Hourly

Hourly suits diagnostic work: a conversion audit, a technical SEO review, a one-time consult on platform selection. The scope is bounded by the deliverable, so hours are a fair proxy for value.

Project or fixed fee

Fixed fees suit defined builds: a platform migration, a checkout redesign, a marketplace storefront launch. Clients prefer a fixed number for budgeting, and it rewards a fast consultant instead of penalizing them. The fee only holds up if deliverables and exclusions are written down before work starts.

Retainer

Retainers suit ongoing work: continuous CRO testing, marketplace account management, seasonal promotion planning, or acting as the outside expert a smaller team checks in with monthly. The value compounds over the relationship rather than showing up in any single week, which is what makes retainers hard to price by the hour.

Performance or revenue share

Some consultants tie part of their fee to a metric: a percentage of incremental revenue after a redesign, or a bonus tied to conversion rate lift. This works when the consultant controls most of the relevant levers, and works poorly when traffic quality, ad spend, or product pricing sit outside the engagement and still move the number the consultant gets judged on.

The classic mistake

The most common pricing mistake in this field is agreeing to a revenue-share or bonus structure without controlling the inputs that drive it. A consultant might rebuild a checkout flow and lift conversion rate, but if the client cuts ad spend the same month, total revenue can still fall, leaving the consultant unpaid for real, measurable work. The safer version ties the bonus to a metric the consultant influences, like conversion rate or cart abandonment, rather than to top-line revenue, which depends on marketing spend the consultant does not control.

A close second is quoting a migration as a flat fee without writing down what happens when the client asks for changes during launch week. Migrations tend to surface real problems on the first day of live traffic, and urgent requests during that window either eat the consultant's margin or become the seed of a dispute.

Using the calculator

Set experience and specialization based on platform certifications and marketplace track record, not just years in the field: a consultant with two years of focused Amazon Advertising work often prices higher than a generalist with eight years of general web consulting. Run the numbers once for hourly diagnostic work and again for a retainer scenario, since the two outputs answer different questions, one a rate for bounded hours, the other a monthly value for a standing relationship. If quoting a fixed project fee, use the hourly output as a floor and build in a buffer for the launch-week support most migrations end up needing.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average e-commerce consultant rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This works out the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Published averages are useful only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20 to 30 hours a week once sales, admin, and unpaid work are taken out. Start with a realistic figure, because a number that is too high sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients buy outcomes rather than hours.
