In 2026, freelance social media managers typically charge around $25-50/hour when starting out and $60-120+/hour once experienced, with monthly retainers commonly running $500-1,500 for a basic one-to-two-platform package and $2,000-5,000+ for full-service work. Rates still vary widely by region, niche, and scope.

What actually moves your rate

Social media pricing rarely comes down to "posts per week." What clients pay for is the stack of skills and time behind each post. The levers that push your number up:

  • Platform count. Each network is its own job: different specs, formats, audiences, and posting windows. Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn and a Facebook page is four content strategies, not one.
  • Short-form video. Reels, TikToks and Shorts are the biggest cost driver in 2026. Scripting, filming and editing vertical video is worth far more than resizing a static graphic, and should be priced apart from feed posts.
  • Community management. Replying to comments and DMs, moderating, and engaging from the account is invisible, unbounded work, and where most managers lose hours without noticing. Put a real number on it.
  • Strategy vs. execution. A client who wants positioning, a hook framework, and monthly analysis pays more than one who just needs their queue filled.
  • Content production. Shooting and editing original assets is a premium over posting client-supplied photos.
  • Paid ads. Running Meta or TikTok campaigns is a separate specialty, usually priced as a management fee plus a percentage of ad spend.
  • Niche and risk. Regulated or technical fields (medical, legal, finance, B2B SaaS) command premiums because the copy carries more consequence.

Why a monthly retainer fits social work best

Social media management is ongoing, relationship-driven, and impossible to time-box honestly. That's what makes the monthly retainer the right default. It gives you predictable income, lets you batch content, and stops you itemizing every "quick story." Hourly billing punishes you for getting fast and makes clients flinch at the ten minutes you spent handling a troll. Reserve hourly rates for audits, one-off consulting, and clearly scoped extras; reserve project pricing for launches and campaigns. Once you can tie work to results (leads, sales, followers that convert), a performance bonus on top of the retainer is fair, but never make your whole fee hostage to an algorithm you don't control.

The classic mistake: the retainer that balloons over time

A flat retainer with no walls guts social media managers more than a low starting rate ever does. It begins as "two platforms, 12 posts a month." Then the client adds a third platform, asks for Reels "since you're already filming," wants you in the DMs daily, and books a weekly call, all for the same fee. Every deliverable, platform, revision round, and response-time expectation belongs in the scope, with a stated rate for anything beyond it. Charge a separate onboarding fee for the front-loaded first month, and raise rates on existing clients at least once a year.

How to use this calculator

Start from your target take-home and let the calculator back into an hourly figure that accounts for unbillable time: the trend-scrolling, the client Slack, the admin. Use that hourly number as your internal costing tool, then package it into a per-client monthly retainer based on platforms, post volume, video, and community management. Quote the retainer, not the hours.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average social media manager rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This calculates the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use any published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Start realistic; a too-high number sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients buy outcomes, not hours.
