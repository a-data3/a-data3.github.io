In 2026, proofreading tends to run about $0.02 to $0.04 per word or $20 to $35 an hour, while copyediting and line editing run roughly $0.03 to $0.09 per word or $30 to $60 an hour, though this varies widely by region, subject specialty, and experience.

How proofreaders and editors should price their work

Proofreading and editing cover a wider range of clients than most people expect: self-published novelists, graduate students finishing a thesis, small businesses that need a website rewritten, publishers preparing a manuscript for print, medical and legal offices that cannot afford a wrong word in a document, and marketing teams sending out a report under their own name. Each of those clients is really buying a different service, even when they all use the word "editing."

Know which service you are actually selling

Proofreading catches typos, punctuation, and formatting errors on a document that is otherwise finished. Copyediting goes further: grammar, consistency, house style, and factual flags, usually checked against a style guide like APA, Chicago, or AP. Line editing works at the sentence level, improving flow, word choice, and clarity. Developmental editing works on structure, argument, or plot before any of that matters. These are different skills with different time demands, and they should carry different rates. A client asking for "editing" often means proofreading and a light copyedit combined, so find out which one you are actually being asked to do before you quote.

What moves the rate

  • Subject matter. Medical, legal, financial, and academic material commands more per word because mistakes carry real consequences and the editor needs to understand the content, not just the sentences.
  • Condition of the draft. A clean manuscript from an experienced writer takes a fraction of the time of one that needs correction on every page, even when the two documents are the same length.
  • Style guide and language variant. Working to a specific style guide, or switching between US and UK English, adds a layer of checking that plain proofreading does not.
  • Turnaround. A same-week or 48-hour deadline is worth a rush surcharge, since it displaces other work and usually means evenings or weekends.
  • Volume and repeat work. A publisher or agency sending a steady stream of manuscripts each month can reasonably negotiate a lower per-word rate in exchange for guaranteed, recurring pipeline.

Pick the pricing model that fits the job

Per-word or per-page rates are the standard for this field. Clients think in word counts, and a per-word rate makes quotes easy to compare across editors. Use it for proofreading and copyediting jobs where you can estimate your pace from a sample. Reserve hourly billing for work you cannot scope in advance, such as developmental editing conversations, academic coaching sessions, or a manuscript in such rough shape that a flat rate would be guesswork. A flat project fee works well once you have read a sample and know roughly how long the full manuscript will take, and authors and students usually prefer a single number to an open-ended hourly estimate. Retainers suit publishers, content agencies, or companies that need a fixed amount of editorial capacity every month, trading a modest discount for predictable income and priority on your calendar.

The mistake that costs editors the most

The recurring error in this field is quoting a rate from a word count alone, without reading any of the actual manuscript. A 90,000-word novel from a careful writer and a 90,000-word novel from a first-time author can take very different numbers of hours, and word count alone tells you nothing about which one you are looking at. Editors who quote sight unseen routinely end up working for far less than their target hourly rate once the messy draft lands in their inbox. Ask for a sample chapter or a handful of representative pages before you quote anything, then do a short, timed edit at the level of service the client wants. Use that pace, not the total word count, to build your price.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average proofreader and editor rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This works out the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Published averages are useful only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20 to 30 hours a week once sales, admin, and unpaid work are taken out. Start with a realistic figure, because a number that is too high sets your rate too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients buy outcomes rather than hours.
