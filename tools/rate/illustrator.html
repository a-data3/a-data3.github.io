In 2026, freelance illustrators typically charge roughly $25–$150 per hour (or about $150–$5,000+ per project, with commercial licensing added on top), though rates vary widely by region, sector, and experience.

How illustrators actually set their rates

Illustration is one of the only freelance fields where the same drawing can be worth $300 or $30,000, and the gap has almost nothing to do with how long it took. What you are really selling is a license to use the artwork, and the price of that license depends on usage far more than on hours. A spot illustration for a niche newsletter and a character that ends up on national packaging might take the same afternoon to draw. They aren't the same job, so they shouldn't carry the same fee.

The factors that move your number

  • Usage and reach. Where the art appears (a personal blog, a billboard, a global campaign) and how large the audience is. This matters more than anything else on this list.
  • Exclusivity and duration. An exclusive, buy-everything license or a multi-year term should cost far more than one-time editorial use.
  • Client sector. Editorial outlets and indie authors pay the least. Advertising, brands, and tech routinely pay 2–10x more for comparable work.
  • Complexity and deliverables. The number of figures, level of detail, color versus line, source files, and format variations.
  • Turnaround and revisions. Rush jobs and open-ended revision rounds both deserve a premium.
  • Demand for your style. A recognizable, hard-to-replace look earns more than a generic one.

Hourly, project, or licensing?

Pure hourly pricing works against you: the faster and more skilled you get, the less you earn for the same result. Use an hourly rate internally to sanity-check your effort, but quote clients a flat project fee built from two parts: the labor to create the work, plus a separate usage license priced to the client's reach. For clients who need a steady stream of illustrations, a monthly retainer smooths your income. For publishing, expect an advance against royalties rather than a one-off fee. Keep day rates for studio-style concept or embedded in-house work.

The classic mistake: selling copyright by accident

The most expensive habit in illustration is handing over full rights ("all rights," "work for hire," or "buyout") for the price of a single use. Once you assign copyright, the client can reuse and resell your art forever, and you can never license it again. Charge a clear premium for buyouts and exclusivity, and by default grant only the specific rights a project needs. Two more leaks cost illustrators real money: quoting without a revision limit (cap it at two or three rounds and bill beyond that), and skipping a kill fee that lets you still collect 25–50% when a project is canceled partway through.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the average illustrator rate?

No. Averages hide too much. This works out the rate you need from your own income goal, costs, and billable hours. Use published averages only as a sanity check against your number.

How many hours can I really bill?

Most full-time freelancers bill 20–30 hours a week once you subtract sales, admin, and unpaid work. Start with a realistic number. If you set it too high, your rate comes out too low.

Should I show clients this hourly rate?

Treat it as your private floor and quote fixed project prices where you can. Clients are paying for the finished work, not for the hours behind it.
