Freelance Contract Clause Library

Copy-and-paste contract clauses in plain English, grouped by what they do. Each one is a starting point you adapt: swap the bracketed placeholders for your project, delete what you don't need, and drop the rest into your agreement. Pair them with the invoice generator and the late-payment calculator once the work is underway.

Not legal advice. These are plain-language starting points, not a finished contract. Contract, tax, and employment law vary by country and by state or province, and the right wording depends on your situation. Copy a clause, adapt every [bracketed] placeholder, and have any important or high-value contract reviewed by a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction before you rely on it.

How to use this page. Read the short note above each clause to check it fits your situation, then click Copy clause and paste it into your document. Anything in square brackets is meant to be replaced with your own details: amounts, dates, party names, and percentages. A useful freelance agreement usually pulls one clause from most of the categories below, plus the basics that tie them together: both parties' names and addresses, the date, the total fee, and a signature line for each side. If you are putting a whole agreement together for the first time, read the contract basics guide first; it explains which clauses matter and when to walk away.

Payment terms

These clauses set out how much you are paid, when, and what happens if payment is late. They are the ones that most directly decide whether you get paid on time.

Due date & Net terms

Use when you want a concrete, chase-able deadline instead of the vague "due on receipt."

Deposit / upfront payment

Use for new clients or larger projects, so you are not funding the work out of your own pocket.

Late-payment interest

This adds a real cost to paying late, so the due date is harder to ignore. Rates and caps are set by local law, so check what your jurisdiction allows, and use the late-payment calculator to work out what an overdue invoice is owed.

Expenses

Use when a project may need paid assets, software, hosting, or travel that you don't want to absorb.

Scope

Scope clauses define what you agreed to do, and also what you did not agree to. They are your defence against a project that grows to twice the size for the same fee.

Scope of work

Every other clause depends on this one. Be specific: list deliverables, formats, and what is explicitly excluded.

Scope creep / change requests

Use so extra requests become a priced, approved change instead of unpaid work you feel obliged to do.

Revisions limit

Use to prevent an endless "just one more tweak" loop while still giving the client fair revisions.

Kill fee & cancellation

These clauses cover what happens if the project stops early through no fault of yours. A kill fee compensates you for the time you set aside and the work you could have booked instead.

Kill fee / cancellation

Use on fixed-fee projects and retainers so a cancelled or stalled job still pays for work done plus a share of the rest.

Intellectual property

These clauses settle who owns the finished work and when ownership passes. Tying the transfer to full payment keeps your work as leverage until the invoice is settled.

IP transfer on final payment

Use so the client gets full rights only once they have paid in full.

Portfolio rights

Use to keep the right to show the work you made, while still respecting anything the client marks confidential.

Confidentiality

A short, mutual confidentiality clause covers most freelance work without the weight of a separate NDA. It protects both sides' private information.

Mutual NDA (short form)

Use when you and the client will exchange sensitive information and neither wants a long standalone NDA.

Liability

A cap on liability limits your worst-case exposure if something goes wrong. It is one of the most valuable clauses in any services contract.

Limitation of liability (cap)

Use to cap your total exposure to what the client actually paid you. Some liability cannot be limited by law, so keep the final sentence.

Independent contractor status

This clause states plainly that you are running your own business rather than working as an employee. It matters for taxes and for how the relationship is classified. The wording alone does not settle the question, because classification depends on how you actually work.

Independent contractor status

Use in most freelance agreements. Classification is judged on the real working relationship, so check your local rules and read the freelancer tax basics guide.

Frequently asked questions

Can I really use these clauses for free?

Yes. Copy, paste, and adapt any clause to your own project at no cost. They are plain-language starting points, not a finished contract and not legal advice, so change the bracketed placeholders and check anything important before you rely on it.

Do these clauses work in my country?

They are written in general plain English, but contract, tax, and employment law vary by country and by state or province. Some terms, such as late-payment interest rates or how independent-contractor status is judged, are governed by local rules. Have a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction review any high-value or important contract.

How do I turn these clauses into a full contract?

Pick the clauses you need, then add the basics that hold them together: the names and addresses of both parties, the project or agreement date, the total fee, and a signature line for each side. Keep the whole thing in writing and make sure both parties sign before work starts.
